Elmira City School District, NY · Case study

An “aha” moment that changed special education district-wide

The situation

New York State kept citing this low-income district for improvement in different areas, from the reading scores of students with IEPs to graduation rates. “It was like playing a game of whack-a-mole,” Dr. Carr says. Many general education teachers didn’t feel prepared to teach neurodivergent students.

What they tried

A PINE pilot at four elementary schools. In 2020–21, 240 educators completed 1,844 hours of self-paced coursework, and PINE office hours gave teams live coaching on specific students. In year two, all 11 district schools joined, including middle and high schools.

What changed

Teachers started offering students choices in how to show what they know, like a report, a poem or a painting. Self-contained classrooms fell to just three in 2022–23, and graduation rates for students with disabilities met the state benchmark.

“I’m seeing the systemic change I was really targeting. The vocabulary we use. How we speak about the students. How we look at problems.”

Dr. Joyce Carr, Supervisor of Special Education and Support Services

Greenwich Public Schools, CT · Case study

From “inclusionary in name only” to real inclusion

The situation

Students with disabilities were in general education classrooms, but often sat on the side working only with their aides. A 2021 outside audit called the classes “inclusionary in name only.” Teachers were short on time, and each of the 11 elementary schools had very different needs.

What they tried

Leaders at every school went through the PINE Accelerator and built a plan for their own school. Some focused on paras, some on special area teachers, some on live office hours. Leaders made clear that PINE was a support, not a mandate.

What changed

Within three months, staff were shifting away from deficit thinking. A speech-language pathologist changed how she writes goals and designs services. Paras began de-escalating and offering breaks. Staff logged more than 40 hours of coaching between November and March.

“It gives them the language to explain that all our bodies and brains just work differently.”

Dr. Stacey Heiligenthaler, Chief Officer of Special Education and Student Supports

Minoa Elementary, East Syracuse Minoa CSD, NY · Case study

From chasing a runaway student to asking why he ran

The situation

The K–5 school was seeing more autistic students and students with ADHD. Staff struggled to understand why a child who reads beautifully might melt down or run from the building. “We fall back on the traditional things we’ve done in the past… and then nothing changes,” says Principal Gerst.

What they tried

The school set aside an hour each month for PINE coursework during the school day and hired substitutes to cover classes. Staff started with the Understanding Autism course, talked it through at faculty meetings and attended the PINE Summit.

What changed

Teachers began presuming competence and building on interests, like math problems about the Titanic for a student who loves it. They now read “misbehavior” as communication and address triggers like loud noises or busy decor.

“This program is good for all kids. We haven’t even scratched the surface yet.”

Gary Gerst, Principal
