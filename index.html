Elmira City School District, NY · Case study

An “aha” moment that changed special education district-wide

The situation

New York State kept citing this low-income district for improvement in different areas, from the reading scores of students with IEPs to graduation rates. “It was like playing a game of whack-a-mole,” Dr. Carr says. Many general education teachers didn’t feel prepared to teach neurodivergent students.

What they tried

A PINE pilot at four elementary schools. In 2020–21, 240 educators completed 1,844 hours of self-paced coursework, and PINE office hours gave teams live coaching on specific students. In year two, all 11 district schools joined, including middle and high schools.

What changed

Teachers started offering students choices in how to show what they know, like a report, a poem or a painting. Self-contained classrooms fell to just three in 2022–23, and graduation rates for students with disabilities met the state benchmark.

“I’m seeing the systemic change I was really targeting. The vocabulary we use. How we speak about the students. How we look at problems.”

Dr. Joyce Carr, Supervisor of Special Education and Support Services

Greenwich Public Schools, CT · Case study

From “inclusionary in name only” to real inclusion

The situation

Students with disabilities were in general education classrooms, but often sat on the side working only with their aides. A 2021 outside audit called the classes “inclusionary in name only.” Teachers were short on time, and each of the 11 elementary schools had very different needs.

What they tried

Leaders at every school went through the PINE Accelerator and built a plan for their own school. Some focused on paras, some on special area teachers, some on live office hours. Leaders made clear that PINE was a support, not a mandate.

What changed

Within three months, staff were shifting away from deficit thinking. A speech-language pathologist changed how she writes goals and designs services. Paras began de-escalating and offering breaks. Staff logged more than 40 hours of coaching between November and March.

“It gives them the language to explain that all our bodies and brains just work differently.”

Dr. Stacey Heiligenthaler, Chief Officer of Special Education and Student Supports

Minoa Elementary, East Syracuse Minoa CSD, NY · Case study

From chasing a runaway student to asking why he ran

The situation

The K–5 school was seeing more autistic students and students with ADHD. Staff struggled to understand why a child who reads beautifully might melt down or run from the building. “We fall back on the traditional things we’ve done in the past… and then nothing changes,” says Principal Gerst.

What they tried

The school set aside an hour each month for PINE coursework during the school day and hired substitutes to cover classes. Staff started with the Understanding Autism course, talked it through at faculty meetings and attended the PINE Summit.

What changed

Teachers began presuming competence and building on interests, like math problems about the Titanic for a student who loves it. They now read “misbehavior” as communication and address triggers like loud noises or busy decor.

“This program is good for all kids. We haven’t even scratched the surface yet.”

Gary Gerst, Principal

Every role matters

A custodian changes how he speaks, and a behavior stops

The situation

One school decided every staff member would take part in PINE: teachers, aides, office staff and custodians.

What changed

A custodian realized that the way he was talking to one student was triggering a repeated behavior. He changed his approach, and the student’s behavior improved a lot.

Growing independence

From needing an aide all day to advocating for himself

The situation

Before PINE, staff described students running, not talking and screaming, and said they didn’t know what to do about it.

What changed

One student who always needed a one-on-one aide no longer needs one. He advocates for himself, reads at almost grade level and has no behavior issues. Staff said they were sad to see him graduate.

Paraphrased from an interview with a school administrator, NYU Usability Lab, 2023.

Building on interests

Turning a “distraction” into a reward

The situation

A student loved Simba from The Lion King, and staff used to see it as something that pulled him off task. They thought interests like this shouldn’t be encouraged.

What changed

After a PINE module on interests, his team made Simba cards and a Velcro schedule he could move Simba on and off of. “Simba time” became something he could see coming, paired with puzzles and other activities.

Paraphrased from an interview with a school administrator, NYU Usability Lab, 2023.

What a PINE membership includes

Ongoing support, not a one-off workshop

True inclusion takes ongoing learning. A PINE membership supports your whole school or district all year.

  1. Leadership training (Accelerator)

    Over four weekly live sessions, your school team gets organized, looks honestly at current practices, sets goals and plans the rollout.

    96% of teams left with a plan they could measure. See dates

  2. Online coursework

    Self-paced courses for every role, from teachers and paras to custodians. Each one is co-created with autistic and neurodivergent people.

    94% say it applies to all of their students, not just those identified as neurodivergent.

  3. Virtual consultation

    “Office hours” with PINE consultants, where teams bring real challenges with specific students and work through them together.

  4. Downloadable tools

    Ready-to-use classroom supports and planning tools that staff can put to work right away.

  5. Professional development activities

    Discussion guides and activities for staff meetings and working groups, so teams can learn together even when time is tight.

  6. PINE Summit

    An annual virtual conference with neurodivergent self-advocates and educators. Sessions are recorded so you can watch later.

    100% of 2024 attendees would recommend it. See sessions

Small changes, real difference

What inclusion can look like

Inclusion doesn’t mean a perfect classroom. It’s a set of everyday practices that make school work for more students.

☀︎▤☺✓

Clear, visual routines

Students know what’s coming next, so there are fewer surprises and smoother transitions for everyone.

More than one way to show learning

In Elmira, students can show what they know through a report, a poem or a painting.

Spaces and tools to regulate

Calming corners, fidgets, headphones, dimmer lights and wait time help students be ready to learn.

Common concerns

The questions we hear most

These are real challenges, and we’d rather talk about them openly.

Our teachers don’t have time for this.

Time is the biggest hurdle every school names. The schools that do best build PINE into the school day instead of adding it after hours. Some examples:

  • Minoa Elementary blocks out an hour a month and hires substitutes to cover classes
  • Elmira made coursework part of teachers’ regular PD schedule, so staff choose when to log in
  • Some schools hold 20-minute team segments where a few engaged staff share key takeaways

The Accelerator helps your team figure out which approach fits your schedule.

How do we justify the cost?

We know budgets are tight, especially now that COVID-era funding has ended. We can talk through what you get in Year 1 and Year 2, show you participation and outcome data, and help you find grants and other financial resources. Ask us about options that fit your district’s size and budget.

Isn’t this just one more thing on teachers’ plates?

PINE is meant to replace guesswork, not add work. In Greenwich, leaders made clear that PINE was “a support and not just another initiative or to-do,” and teachers who saw it help spread the word to colleagues.

Does this work in middle and high school?

Every grade level and setting is different, and we don’t pretend one approach fits all. In Elmira, all 11 schools, including middle and high schools, use PINE. Tell us about your students and we’ll talk through how it could work for you.

How are neurodivergent people actually involved?

The autistic community is an active partner and guide in creating every PINE course. Neurodivergent experts help develop and review the content, and self-advocates speak at the PINE Summit.

Can we talk to a school that already uses PINE?

Yes. New schools often find this the most helpful step. Tell us about your school and we’ll connect you with an administrator in a similar setting.
